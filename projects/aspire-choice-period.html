Hathaway Brown School · Pro Bono Project

Aspire Choice Period Assignment System

A preference-driven class assignment system built for Hathaway Brown's Aspire program — every day, students submit their choices through a Form, and the system turns them into a full, capacity-respecting assignment in minutes, automating a process a volunteer previously had to run by hand, every day, for the length of the program. I led the three-person team that built it and was the school's primary point of contact throughout.

Google Apps Script Maximum Bipartite Matching Team Leadership Stakeholder Communication

The Problem

A daily assignment problem, done by hand every day

Aspire is a Hathaway Brown program that works with middle school girls in Greater Cleveland who are navigating academic hardship and working toward being the first in their families to attend college, taught in part by high school and college students under the mentorship of experienced educators. During its daily Choice Period, participants pick from a set of elective sessions, each with a hard cap on how many students it can hold — and someone has to turn that stack of preferences into a full, capacity-respecting room assignment before class starts, every single day the program runs. Before this system, a volunteer did that by hand every day, and it was slow and imperfect — exactly the kind of repetitive, error-prone process that doesn't hold up over weeks of daily repetition.

The Approach

A workflow simple enough for anyone to run

I designed the whole system around a rule: the more invisible the technology, the better. Each day, the administrator opened a Google Form, and students submitted their choices through it — the same kind of form they'd used for anything else at the program, nothing new to learn. Every response landed in a Google Sheet, and Apps Script did the rest: validating the incoming responses, running the matching engine, and sharing out the finished class assignments for students and staff to see. What used to be a volunteer's daily task became a single click, repeated every day the program ran.

Technical Implementation

Choosing the matching algorithm

The core problem is a bipartite graph: students on one side, sessions on the other, with an edge for every session a student ranked and a hard capacity on every session node. The actual goal was to fill every session while giving as many students as possible one of their higher-ranked choices — which meant the right formulation was a maximum matching problem, not a two-sided stable matching like a residency match. Sessions don't have preferences of their own, so the extra machinery a Gale-Shapley-style algorithm brings wasn't buying anything; a matching algorithm that directly maximized the number of students placed into their best available choice, subject to capacity, was the more honest model of the actual objective and was far simpler to implement correctly in Apps Script within Google's execution-time limits.

The engine works through each student's ranked list in order, using augmenting paths to place students into their next-available preference whenever a session is full, so a later, lower-priority student doesn't block an earlier one out of a session it could still reach through a different path. Because the whole thing runs against a few hundred students rather than thousands, it re-solves from scratch in well under a minute — fast enough that the liaison could change a capacity or add late responses and just run it again.

Edge Cases

Designing for students who try to break it

Once a few students realized the system existed, some of them tried to game it — and the Form had to be treated as an adversarial input, not a clean data source. Students submitted the form more than once, which meant the engine had to always resolve to a single, latest response per student rather than quietly summing duplicates. Some left preferences blank, ranked the same session twice, or submitted after a session had already filled hoping to get bumped in ahead of someone else. A few figured out that listing only one "safe" choice guaranteed them a seat there rather than risking a more competitive option elsewhere, which meant the matching logic had to treat an incomplete preference list as a real, deliberate input rather than an error to reject.

Before the system ever touched a real response, I wrote a battery of synthetic test cases that mirrored exactly this behavior — duplicate submissions, blank and repeated rankings, late entries, capacity ties — and ran the matching engine against all of them until every case resolved to one clean, valid assignment. That testing pass caught more real problems than anything in the "happy path" version of the system ever would have.

Leading the Team

Delegating, tracking, and being the point of contact

I led a three-person team through the build and split ownership along clear lines: I owned the matching engine and the Apps Script core, and delegated the Form/response-sheet setup and the validation-and-testing pass to the other two, with regular check-ins to track progress and unblock whichever piece was behind. I was also the sole point of contact with the school's project liaison — taking requirements that were sometimes informal or non-technical (a grade-level priority rule, a hard cap that changed midway through), translating them into concrete features, and reporting progress and any blockers back in terms the liaison could act on without needing to understand how the system worked underneath.

Rolling It Out

Teaching a system to the people who'd actually run it

Building the system was only half the job — it also had to work without me in the room. I walked the liaison and the rest of the staff through the live Form and Sheet, showing them exactly how to read the generated assignment, re-run it after new responses came in, and handle an exception by hand if something looked off, all without touching Apps Script or needing to understand the matching algorithm behind it. The whole workflow was built to be boring and repeatable on purpose, so the school could run it again next cycle on their own.

Results

Impact

The system replaced a volunteer's slow, imperfect, by-hand process with one that regenerates a full, capacity-respecting class assignment in minutes, every single day the program ran — and it held up against real students actively trying to find its edge cases. What I Learned

Reflections

  • Being the single point of contact is its own skill. Translating an informal, non-technical ask from a school liaison into a concrete feature — and translating progress back the other way — mattered as much as anything I wrote in Apps Script.
  • Teaching a system is different from building it. The version staff could run on their own without me had to be simpler and more boring than the version I'd have built for myself.
  • The right algorithm follows from the actual objective. It was tempting to reach for a more familiar two-sided matching approach; reasoning through what was actually being optimized — filling every seat, honoring rank, respecting capacity — pointed to a simpler, more direct answer.
  • Real users will find every edge your test cases missed. Designing synthetic test cases around how students would actually try to game the form caught problems no happy-path test ever surfaced.
