Inspection Assignment & Optimization

A Python-based optimization system that assigns approximately 5,000 field inspections twice annually while balancing geographic proximity, inspector workload, preferences, and operational constraints. The system reduced assignment turnaround from days to hours and incorporates LLM-assisted analysis of unstructured inspector preferences.

Python pandas geopy SQLite Optimization Excel Automation AI-Assisted

The Problem

Thousands of inspections, without a unified optimization model

CMCS assigns roughly 5,000 inspections across its inspector network twice a year. Before this system, a tool already generated those assignments, but it did not weigh geographic proximity, inspector workload, and scheduling preferences together, so the results were often inefficient, parts of the process remained tedious to run, and a full cycle still took multiple days without scaling as inspection volume grew.

The Approach

An optimization-first assignment engine

I designed the system as a constrained optimization problem rather than a sequence of manual assignment rules. Geographic proximity, workload, inspection count, availability, and inspector preferences are incorporated into the assignment process, with the resulting schedules generated directly in a format the operations team can use. LLM-assisted components are used to interpret unstructured preference notes alongside the deterministic optimization logic (see below).

Technical Implementation

How it works

  • Data ingestion: inspection records (location, type, constraints) loaded and cleaned with pandas from an Excel workbook.
  • Geocoding & geographic processing: addresses resolved to coordinates with geopy, and distances between inspectors and inspection sites computed to drive the geography objective. Each inspector's home base, coverage range, and local-vs.-travel split are visible directly on the map.
  • Clustering & grouped assignment: travel inspections outside every nearby inspector's range are grouped into clusters, so a whole cluster of nearby work can be assigned to one inspector in a single pass instead of one inspection at a time.
  • Workload balancing: each inspector's current load and preferences modeled as constraints so assignments don't overload any one person.
  • LLM-assisted preference ranking: uses LLM APIs (Claude, Gemini) to read free-text notes the person running the assignment writes in for a given inspector — an inspector who doesn't want to work the south side of Chicago, or one who lives in an RV and travels a self-described route — and folds that directly into how candidates are ranked for a given cluster, alongside stated availability and other preferences.
  • Assignment / optimization: the assignment problem is formulated as a variant of the Generalized Assignment Problem (GAP), with each inspection assigned to exactly one inspector while maximizing an overall fit score subject to simultaneous capacity constraints on total workdays and inspection count. The production implementation uses a custom constructive and local-search heuristic rather than an off-the-shelf MILP solver. A fairness-aware extension adds dual-ordering bin-packing logic and a rescue pass to reduce uneven capacity fragmentation across inspectors.
  • Output: results written directly to formatted Excel schedules, so the operations team could act on them without manual re-entry.

Results

Impact

The pipeline reduced assignment turnaround from multiple days to hours while incorporating geographic proximity, workload balance, and inspector preferences into the assignment process. It is now used to generate assignments for approximately 5,000 inspections on a semiannual cycle.

Engineering Lessons

Reflections

  • Formalizing a people-facing process takes as much time as implementing it. Translating an operational workflow into a formal optimization problem required as much time understanding constraints as it did writing code.
  • Competing objectives require explicit tradeoffs. Balancing distance, workload, and preference meant real tradeoffs, not an "optimize everything" objective.
  • The best design input often comes from the people running the process. Discussing the most tedious, error-prone parts of the old workflow with the coworker who ran it every cycle shaped what the system should actually optimize, rather than simply re-automating the old tool's steps one-for-one.
