Inspection Assignment & Optimization

An AI-assisted Python optimization engine that assigns roughly 5,000 field inspections to inspectors twice a year — balancing geography, workload, preferences, and operational constraints — cutting assignment turnaround from days to hours.

Python pandas geopy SQLite Optimization Excel Automation AI-Assisted

The Problem

Thousands of inspections, a tool that didn't optimize

CMCS assigns roughly 5,000 inspections across its inspector network twice a year. Before this system, a tool already generated those assignments, but it didn't weigh geographic proximity, inspector workload, and scheduling preferences together — so the results were often inefficient, parts of the process stayed tedious to run, and a full cycle still took multiple days without scaling as inspection volume grew.

The Approach

An optimization-first assignment engine

I designed a Python-based system that treats assignment as a real optimization problem — encoding geography, workload, and preferences as constraints and objectives, then producing assignments the operations team can act on directly, with no manual re-entry. It also layers in AI-assisted components alongside the core optimization logic (see below).

Data ingestion → Geocoding → Clustering → Assignment / optimization → Excel output

Inspection Assignment Map — a live run: 2,550 inspections across 81 inspectors, split into local coverage and remaining travel work, with adjustable per-inspector range and cluster markers grouping nearby travel inspections together.

Technical Implementation

How it works

  • Data ingestion: inspection records (location, type, constraints) loaded and cleaned with pandas from an Excel workbook.
  • Geocoding & geographic processing: addresses resolved to coordinates with geopy, and distances between inspectors and inspection sites computed to drive the geography objective. Each inspector's home base, coverage range, and local-vs.-travel split are visible directly on the map.
  • Clustering & grouped assignment: travel inspections outside every nearby inspector's range are grouped into clusters, so a whole cluster of nearby work can be assigned to one inspector in a single pass instead of one inspection at a time.
  • Workload balancing: each inspector's current load and preferences modeled as constraints so assignments don't overload any one person.
  • AI-assisted preference ranking: uses LLM APIs (Claude, Gemini) to read free-text notes the person running the assignment writes in for a given inspector — an inspector who doesn't want to work the south side of Chicago, or one who lives in an RV and travels a self-described route — and folds that directly into how candidates are ranked for a given cluster, alongside stated availability and other preferences.
  • Assignment / optimization: a hand-rolled construction + local-search heuristic for the Generalized Assignment Problem (GAP) — each inspection is assigned to exactly one inspector, maximizing total fit score subject to two capacity ceilings enforced simultaneously (total workdays and total inspection count). The production path runs a fairness-aware variant on top of the base solver, adding dual-ordering bin-packing fill and a rescue pass so capacity doesn't fragment unevenly across inspectors. It's hand-rolled instead of built on an off-the-shelf MILP solver (PuLP/OR-Tools) since neither could be installed in the environment this was built in — a proper solver is the natural next upgrade.
  • Output: results written directly to formatted Excel schedules, so the operations team could act on them without manual re-entry.

Results

Impact

The new pipeline cuts assignment turnaround from multiple days to hours, and reduces the wasted travel and workload imbalance the old tool had no way to account for. Exact dollar savings aren't tracked yet, but less unnecessary travel and rework is a direct cost reduction for CMCS.

What I Learned

Reflections

  • Translating a people-facing operational process into a formal optimization problem took as much time understanding constraints as it did writing code.
  • Balancing competing objectives — distance, workload, preference — meant real tradeoffs, not just "optimize everything."
  • Some of the most useful design work happened away from the keyboard: talking through the most tedious, error-prone parts of the old process with a coworker who ran it every cycle, then turning that into a concrete plan for what the system should actually optimize — not just re-automate the old tool's steps one-for-one.
