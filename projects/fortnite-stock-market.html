Forecast: a Fantasy Stock Market for Competitive Fortnite

Forecast is a fantasy stock market for competitive Fortnite. Professional players are issued as tradeable stocks, priced through a live order book, and pay dividends when they place well in tournaments — giving people who follow the competitive scene a market where that knowledge has real value.

The Idea

A market built around real trading mechanics

Professional Fortnite players are issued as stocks through an auction, then trade on a live order book the same way an equity trades on an exchange. When a player places well in a tournament, a share of the prize pool is paid out per share as a dividend, and cash that isn't invested in players sits in a treasury instrument and earns yield. The goal was to reward genuine insight into the competitive scene, which meant the market itself had to behave like a real one: prices set by actual supply and demand, liquidity that someone has to provide, and settlement that reconciles exactly every time.

How It Works

Order types and how a trade clears

Trading runs on a real order book with two order types.

  • Limit orders rest in the book at a chosen price until matched. Matching follows price-time priority: the best price fills first, and ties go to whichever order arrived first.
  • Quick orders execute immediately, sweeping through resting liquidity at the best available price — the effective price worsens as the order gets larger. If the book alone can't fill it, a liquidity-provider position steps in, capped to its own holdings so it can never sell shares it doesn't have.

Settlement

Exact settlement on every payout

Dividends and auction proceeds are distributed using an exact largest-remainder ("Hamilton") apportionment method — the same rounding-fair approach used in proportional-representation voting — so a fixed pool splits across shareholders without leaking or duplicating a fraction of a cent. Total cash and total shares are designed to stay conserved exactly after every trade, dividend, and settlement.

Liquidity

Liquidity has to come from somewhere

A market with no resting orders has nothing to trade against. A population of automated participants, each following a different trading strategy, keeps the book populated so human traders always have someone on the other side — placing orders through the same path a human trade uses, with no special access.

Testing the Rules

Testing the design before going live

Before running with real users, the market was simulated across many cycles with populations of bot traders to find where the rules broke — runaway inflation, dividends miscalibrated relative to trading activity, fees that starved liquidity instead of supporting it. The fee structure, dividend sizing, and treasury rate in the system today came out of that process, not out of guessing.

What I Learned

Lessons from designing a market, not just an app

  • The rules only become real at the edges. Deciding what happens when a quick order can't find enough liquidity — and capping the fallback so the market can never sell shares it doesn't have — mattered more than the happy-path trading logic.
  • Every parameter is coupled to every other one. Fee rates affect liquidity, dividend size affects wealth distribution, and the treasury rate affects how much cash sits idle — none of them can be tuned in isolation, which is why the economy was simulated in batches before any of them were set for real.
  • The economics have to be decided before the product, not fixed afterward. Deciding the rules up front, even in a simulator, was far cheaper than redesigning core logic after real positions already existed.
