CMCS · Internship Project

Automated Email Generation System

A Python system that fills PDF templates from Excel data and emails them out on schedule — replacing a fully manual process across four separate mailings and saving an estimated 15–20 hours of work per day, or about $40,000 a year, across five schedulers.

Python PDF Automation Email Automation Excel Automation

The Problem

A manual process nobody had gotten around to fixing

I started that summer as a data-entry intern, manually filling in PDF forms from Excel data and passing them to schedulers, who reviewed and emailed them out to clients by hand. Several other people across the team did the same kind of work every day, across several different scheduled mailings, and nobody had automated it or seriously looked into whether it could be.

The Approach

From spreadsheet to sent email, with no one in the loop

With only a few classes of coding experience, I proposed automating the process to my supervisor and built a system that reads the same Excel data used in the manual version, populates the PDF template directly, and sends it by email — all without anyone opening a file. Once the first mailing worked end to end, I extended the same pattern to all four scheduled email types the team had been sending out manually.

Excel data → Populate PDF template → Build email from template → Attach & send

Results

A small project with an outsized impact

This was far less technically involved than the optimization and analytics systems I built later, but it mattered just as much — it replaced a real, recurring cost in people's time that nobody had prioritized fixing. Automating all four scheduled mailings saved an estimated 15–20 hours of combined work per day, or roughly $40,000 a year, across five schedulers, and turned into an extended role building larger systems the following year.

What I Learned

Reflections

  • Pitching the idea mattered as much as building it. Nobody was going to hand a brand-new intern with almost no coding background a project like this — I had to make the case for it myself, on top of the data-entry work I was already assigned.
  • A mistake here wasn't a lower grade on one assignment — it was a real email going out to a real client, wrong or incomplete. The source data was often messy and missing values in inconsistent places, so most of the actual engineering was defensive: validating everything before it touched a template, and building in failsafes that would hold a mailing back rather than let anything imperfect go out.
  • Impact and technical difficulty aren't the same axis. This was the simplest system I've built, and one of the highest-impact, because the value was in noticing an overlooked, repetitive process that nobody had prioritized fixing — not in the sophistication of the fix itself.
